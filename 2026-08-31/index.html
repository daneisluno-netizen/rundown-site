What it left out or got wrong

1. The Wizard of Oz test as described is not safe to run in the UK. Taking a real card number for a product that does not exist, then telling the customer "servers are over capacity", is misrepresentation, and holding card data outside a payment flow is a PCI and UK GDPR problem. He raises none of this — the story is told as a clever hack with no downside. For a UK seller aiming at MSPs, running it his way would be an own goal in the exact market where you are selling trust.

The same signal, safely: a Stripe payment link for a paid pilot or a refundable deposit — the money genuinely moves, you never touch card data, and a refund is a normal operation. That is a stronger validation than his version, because a completed payment beats an abandoned card entry.

2. "Connect your Gmail, Slack, calendar, financials so it knows everything about you" — from a man selling an AI operating system, with no word on data governance. You sell security posture for a living. Whatever you do personally, this is the paragraph that would end the conversation with any client who has a DPA. Worth noticing that the omission is total: not a caveat, not a footnote.

3. Nothing about what happens after the first payment. The whole video ends at one payment from ten hand-picked contacts. That is deliberately the scope, but it means the hard part for you — whether an agentic MSP technician can be delivered repeatably at a margin — is out of frame. Do not mistake clearing his bar for clearing yours.

4. It is a funnel. Three separate DM-me-on-Instagram calls to action for the "AI Company OS", plus a partner pitch. That does not make the mechanism wrong — the mechanism is unusually concrete for this genre — but the free thing is the product and you are the lead.

The one thing worth trying

Run question three this week against the agentic MSP technician idea, before building any more of it.

Concretely: write the one-page offer (promise, timeline, price), list ten MSPs you can actually reach, send the message, and ask for money — a paid pilot, not a call, not a demo. Stripe payment link, no product built.

Your own record says the pattern is stopping at friction and relabelling it closure ([[project-life-direction]]), and that seven ideas have been killed with zero validated ([[project-acquisition]]). Both of those are what "no real payment was ever asked for" looks like from the inside. This is the cheapest possible test of whether idea #8 deserves to exist, and the answer arrives in seven days either way.

Second, smaller: add "now push back on me — tell me why this fails, where am I kidding myself" to the end of your own prompts. You already have /red-team and /second-brain doing this at the session level; he is applying it per-answer, which is cheaper and catches the flattery earlier.

Dr Darby Saxbe on the father's brain (Diary Of A CEO)

Watch 01:08:00–01:16:00. That is the eight minutes with the advice in it.

The other eighty are the science behind why the advice works, and they are genuinely good, but you have three kids — you have already run the experiment.

  • Exploration pick. This episode was rejected on a previous run at 3/10 on the assumption you have no children. [[user]] records three. It was re-scored 6 after grounding and queued; this run is the Monday exploration arm cashing that queue. It is in the gate log as the engine's only recorded miss.
Bottom line

Fatherhood measurably rewires men — about 1% of cortical grey matter lost, concentrated in the social-cognition network, plus roughly a quarter off testosterone — and the shrinkage is adaptive, not damage: the brain is pruning to run one job better. Saxbe's argument is that this is a facultative adaptation, meaning it scales with how hands-on you actually are. Dads who bonded more before birth and did more care afterwards showed more change.

The load-bearing part for you is not the neuroscience. It is two findings that survive outside parenting: the couples who fought with contempt and stonewalling during pregnancy had measurably more complex births months later, and the single ingredient her lab found that flips a negative argument positive is validation — saying "I see where you're coming from" before anything else.

What is evidence and what is anecdote

She is unusually clean on this, and it is the reason the episode is worth the time. The brain and hormone findings are her own lab's longitudinal work with pre-registered-style before/after design. She flags the limits herself — "the human hormone literature is messy", "a lot of these are weak correlations", "I don't know that we know for sure if there's a dose of parenting involvement". That is a scientist, not a guest on a book tour pretending certainty.

But she is on a book tour. Both editions get held up at the end. The framing she is selling — "great fathers are made, not born" — is the book's thesis and every finding is arranged to support it. Nothing looks bent, but the selection is not neutral.

Two places to discount:

  • The birth-complications finding is correlational, and she does not label it. Couples who fight with contempt at six months pregnant differ in a dozen other ways — stress, money, health, support — that also predict complicated births. She describes the mechanism as if the conflict causes the complication. It may; the study as described cannot show it.
  • Bartlett does most of the overclaiming, not her. The oxytocin/Alzheimer's passage, the fertility stat block, "and that's proven by science" about the dad bod — those are his. When a claim in this episode sounds too strong, check which voice said it. She repeatedly walks his framings back.

One genuine omission: an hour and a half on the transition to parenthood and essentially nothing on fathers of older children, beyond one second-hand line from Richard Reeves about paternity leave up to age 18. Every finding is anchored to birth and the first six months. With kids well past that, most of this episode describes a change that already happened to you — which is exactly why the useful eight minutes are the general relationship mechanics, not the neuroscience.

The one thing worth trying

Use validation as the opener in the next disagreement you have — with anyone. "I understand what you're saying, I see where you're coming from" before your counter-argument. Her lab's finding is that this single behaviour is what flips a negative conflict positive, and her description of why is precise: once someone feels heard, the wind goes out of being furious.

That is not really parenting advice. [[user_superdane]] records that your current first domain is communication and listening, and names the pattern you are working against — defaulting to debate and proving merit rather than listening. This is the smallest concrete version of the fix, it is testable this week, and it came out of a controlled conflict task rather than a self-help book.

Secondary, if you want the neuroscience to pay for itself: the "we" versus "I" finding. Couples who talked about the work as ours before it arrived were more satisfied after — controlling for how they felt beforehand. That transfers straight to any shared project with a partner.

What it left out or got wrong

1. The site-level override claim is the wrong way round, and it matters most. He says the new default "would be overriding even the site level settings that you may have introduced". Public coverage of the rollout says the opposite: admins can change the new default on a per-site-collection or per-OneDrive basis. If you tell a client their carefully-set site defaults are about to be steamrolled and they aren't, you look careless; if you tell them nothing changes and the tenant default flips under them, worse. Read MC1454378 in the client's own Message Center before advising either way — this is a tenant-specific rollout and the MC post is authoritative where a YouTube summary is not.

2. He does not say what happens to existing links. The whole risk of a default-sharing change is the back catalogue, not the new shares. Microsoft's own Q&A on changing the default link type indicates existing links keep working as issued — the default only affects new shares. He never addresses it, and it is the first question any client will ask.

3. The EWS item is under-sold. He calls it "a lot to unpack" and moves on. It is a break-with-no-config-change: cross-tenant free/busy today runs on Organization Relationship / Availability Address Space, both EWS-backed, and the replacement is an inbound-only policy that both tenants must configure independently. Any client sharing calendars with a partner org has a dated outage waiting. That is a bigger operational story than the passkey items he spends twice as long on.

4. No mention of the Feb 2027 blocking behaviour in the SMS section — only the September nudge. September is a nudge; 1 February 2027 is when sign-in breaks for users whose only method is SMS or voice, with no tenant opt-out. He briefs the soft date and omits the hard one.
